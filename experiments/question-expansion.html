How did we decide whether to keep a question?

The report assistant checked each draft against the available transcript. This was a source review by an AI assistant, not an independent human rating. A question was kept only when earlier discussion supported its premise and answer, its requester had a plausible reason to ask, and its wording was suitable for speech.

  • Source support: required facts must appear before the proposed question time. A guess stays a guess, and a proposed idea stays a proposal.
  • Requester and wording: the question must fit the participant’s current task. Transcript letters such as A or D must not be spoken as if they were names.
  • Need for earlier discussion: the latest minute must not already contain the complete answer, and the question must not reveal its own answer.
  • Correct proposed answer: numbers, responsibilities and changed plans must match the source. A usable question can need a corrected answer label while its wording stays unchanged.

Three concrete errors, with their meeting context

An assumed cost limit: in TS3003b, the team discusses a docking station—a base that can recharge the TV remote. The model asked what the budget limit for that base had been. The available discussion did not assign a separate cost limit to the base, so the question assumed information that had never been stated.

A transcript letter used as a name: another draft began “D, can you remind us…”. A–D are labels added to identify AMI speakers. D was also the selected requester, so the wording had that participant address their own transcript label.

Two survey percentages confused: in the IS1000b product-design role-play, the marketing participant reports a survey of remote-control users. Fifty percent often lose their remotes; 34% find a new remote hard to learn. In the later generation setting, the report assistant first selected relevant earlier utterances and described the information needed. Even with that help, the model’s proposed answer used 34% for losing the remote. The source-checked answer is 50%.

When would the retained questions be asked?

Across the 23 wordings, the planned question time averages 29.0 minutes after the conversation starts, or 70.3% of the full conversation. The earliest is 16.4 minutes in; the latest is 47.1 minutes in. These are proposed positions in the source recording. This expansion did not insert new audio.

The timing is biased by our selection rule. AMI candidate times were chosen near 56% and 86% of each meeting, then adjusted to pauses. Earnings-call candidates were chosen near 60% and 86% of their timed transcript span. The final percentages use official full conversation durations. No retained question is before 53%; this is not a measured distribution of when real people ask assistants for help.

How the questions and answers were produced

Source selection happens offline and can inspect complete transcript files. The generation and reader models receive only the chosen earlier speech. This study does not measure prediction of future requests. The local generator and reader use the same Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 checkpoint, so their agreement is not an independent check.

The reader was tested on 32 new question drafts and nine unchanged questions from the earlier EN2003a pilot. Each was answered with five versions of the context: all earlier speech; only the latest minute; no transcript; earlier speech without speaker labels; and earlier speech with selected supporting excerpts removed. Question quality and reader accuracy were checked separately. The remote-loss question is clear and has a source-supported answer of 50%, even though the reader gives 34%. Retaining that question does not mean accepting the reader’s wrong answer.

A reader’s failure with only the latest minute also does not prove that older history is needed. In EN2002b, the three search-result types were repeated inside that minute, but the reader still failed to list them. We excluded that early version as a history test. The assistant-written version uses a later time when the recent minute does not contain the list.

All 205 responses were complete machine-readable records, yet 34 cited passages that were not supplied: 33 with no transcript and one after supporting excerpts had been removed. The reader also missed changes to talk capacity and contact responsibilities in the earlier workshop questions. These are reasons to keep checking the transcript directly.

This is a useful example for designing the next review. We should still check the whole earlier discussion for another passage that supplies both facts before treating it as a validated two-step item. Inspect the unchanged question and its source passages.

Proposed direction for discussion

I recommend beginning with a small set of two-step questions. MuSiQue provides the key principle: the second step must depend on the first. CQG and question-rewriting work offer ways to preserve that connection during generation. Min and colleagues show why the final question and supplied evidence must still be checked for shortcuts.

  1. Identify a participant through an earlier task, then recover a separate commitment. Use the task description only when it fits how the requester would naturally refer to that person.
  2. Apply a changed requirement to an earlier plan. Ask about the consequence for the decision currently being discussed. Exclude cases where a later recap already gives the whole answer.
  3. Later, connect call history to a document lookup. Earlier speech should identify the relevant metric or store group; an available document supplies the missing comparison or exact value.

Before keeping a question, check that two separate facts are necessary, search the whole available discussion for a shortcut, and withhold each required fact while accounting for repeated mentions. A plausible alternative person or metric should test whether identifying the right one matters. Wording, requester motivation and ambiguity need their own review. The same reader model’s agreement cannot certify these properties.

A later pilot should include early, middle and late question times where the needed evidence has actually become available. Question position, age of the evidence and number of required reasoning steps should be reported separately.

Technical details and earlier diagnostic attempts

Transcript timing, model settings and what the reader saw

AMI provides manually annotated words and speaker identities. We assumed an extra 0.5 seconds before making a completed excerpt available. EN2002b has no formal participant roles; its requester perspectives come from earlier speech. The other AMI meetings have assigned product-design roles.

For the two earnings calls, ConEC provides corrected words and automatic timing. 4,552 of 15,714 words lack complete start/end boundaries. Words without a final boundary are held until a later timed word provides an anchor. A final excerpt with no later anchor is withheld. This is a text-release convention; earnings audio insertion has not been verified.

Generation and reading use Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 through vLLM: BF16 weights, prefix caching, CUDA graphs, a 49,152-token context and an 8 GiB attention cache. Reader responses allow 1,536 output tokens; the largest checked input is 30,452 tokens. Full participant rosters and words harvested from later speech are not reader inputs.

Removing selected source excerpts can leave equivalent information elsewhere. Removing speaker labels can leave naturally spoken names. These controls must be interpreted alongside source inspection.
