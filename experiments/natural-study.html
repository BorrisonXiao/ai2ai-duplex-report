S1 yields and answers the clarification.

One human-recorded question, one same-speaker clarification during model speech, and S2 entirely absent.

Observed interaction

The human asks about outlet stores. S1 starts its explanation. During that speech, the human asks “Faulty products?”. The model emits a native [STOP], invalidates old queued audio, and gives a new spoken answer. Independent transcription captures the audible cut: “An outlet is a… No, not faulty products. Outlets sell genuine items at discounted prices.”

This demonstrates stopping and clarification uptake in this example. The categorical “not faulty” answer is simplistic; interaction success does not establish comprehensive factual accuracy or general interruption robustness.

Inspect the content

N2a

S1 ASR: Um, what's the difference between an outlet and a regular store?

Actual spoken transcript: An outlet is a store that sells products at a discount often with a different layout and selection than a regular store. The main difference is the pricing strategy.

N2b

S1 ASR: Um, what's the difference between an outlet and a regular store?Faulty products?

Actual spoken transcript: An outlet is a... No, not faulty products. Outlets sell genuine items at discounted prices.

The interrupted model text includes “a store” before STOP, but the audible replay cuts that queued continuation. Actual PCM and source epoch tags, rather than synthesized-but-discarded clips, determine the stopping result.

Source and adaptation

DailyTalk is a human-recorded conversational TTS dataset based on scripted DailyDialog dialogues. These inputs are recorded people, not TTS-generated user speech. We use Kyutai’s pinned stereo release, conversation 656, speaker/channel 1, original turns 2 and 4. The request and clarification are cropped, resampled from 44.1 kHz to 24 kHz, and scheduled relative to actual model playback. No speed, volume, or voice conversion is applied to the user crops.

Independent input transcription recovers all semantic words; it omits the filler “Umm” and question punctuation. The original waveform is retained. Exact checksums and source sample bounds · CC BY-SA 4.0 attribution and adaptation.

What the preliminary controls found

The first dinner proposal was correctly transcribed but silent. Its later latency spikes coincide with dropping the cached history prefix after tick 40; the earlier question itself had p95 0.316 s and negligible backlog. That no-answer behavior is therefore separate from the late cache issue. The new test uses a predefined 40-tick window and a factual request, while the longer-stream cache problem remains open.

Temperature 0.8, used by the authors’ realtime harness, produced a native STOP in a preliminary interruption case, but also an unwanted greeting and requests for disabled S2. Greedy decoding produces the complete human-question answer and the demonstrated clarification response. The final matched pair changes only the actual user clarification after an identical generated prefix.

Runtime and limitations

Final job 1035079 completed on one H100 SXM 80 GB. Thinker, Talker, MTP and Code2Wav share GPU 0; S2 is not loaded. Runtime qualification passed before the interruption. Allocation was 130 s (0.03611 GPU hours); all three actual GPU allocations total 0.13556 GPU hours. No experiment GPU remains active.

  • One human-recorded scripted dialogue; original barge-in timing is adapted, not naturally occurring.
  • Only original user channel is supplied; source counterpart responses are excluded.
  • All waiting gaps and queued-audio decisions remain in full PCM replay. ASR uses one contiguous audible window; no concatenation or time compression.
  • STOP timing is from the first correction packet; actual nonquiet user onset is reported separately and is not acoustic word alignment.
  • S2 is absent. A model THINK request is logged separately from an actual S2 dispatch.
  • Forty-tick capture avoids the identified late rolling-history cache issue; longer streams remain unqualified.
  • Automatic speech transcripts and lexical flags require semantic content review; no robust benchmark success rate is implied.
  • The clarification response is too categorical about outlet-product quality; interaction success does not imply a complete factual evaluation.
