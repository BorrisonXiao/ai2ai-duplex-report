The release describes tests of long conversations, multi-party participation, and bilingual ability. The scope table above distinguishes those content and participation judgments from direct interruption latency, acoustic fluency, and tool-use evaluation.

What actually happens during evaluation?

First, the speech model takes one person’s place in a recorded meeting. Afterwards, a separate AI called Gemma reads the written conversation and judges how well the model participated.

  1. The speech model joins the recording. It hears the other people and produces its own replies. The other people’s speech comes from the original recording, so they keep following their original conversation.
  2. The evaluator writes out the conversation. It combines the model’s words with the other people’s words in time order. In this written version, A is the model, and B, C and so on are the recorded people.
  3. Gemma reads that written conversation six times. Each “call” is one request sent to Gemma, with a different set of questions. The first request asks what part A played in the meeting. The other five ask for ratings of different aspects of A’s behavior.

All six calls read the same conversation and judge A. The first call identifies a meeting role; the sixth uses that role to choose its questions. The five scoring calls give ratings from 1 to 5 and short reasons for those ratings. These ratings are combined into the final score, with a separate adjustment for how far into the meeting A kept participating. See how the score is calculated.

The judge reads words

Gemma is the judge model, google/gemma-4-31B-it. It receives written lines such as “A: We have 400 dollars left.” It is told to use A’s own lines as evidence for A’s performance. The judge does not receive audio or the times when people spoke, so these six calls cannot tell us whether the voice sounded good or whether the model stopped quickly when interrupted.   • Chloe: “We need to reserve 100 dollars for testing.”

The six calls, in everyday language

1 of 6 · Source name: Call 1

What part did A play in the meeting?

The judge first decides how A helped the group. Did A organize the discussion, put forward new ideas, build on other people’s ideas, or point out risks? The benchmark calls these roles Facilitator, Driver, Collaborator and Evaluator.

Example: A says, “Ben, what is our budget? Chloe, what do we need to test? Then let’s agree on the next step.” That suggests a Facilitator: someone helping organize the meeting.

This call returns a role label and an explanation. It does not give the quality ratings used in the final score. A can act like a facilitator and still do that job poorly; the sixth call checks how well it did the job. Read the exact prompt.

2 of 6 · Source name: Call 2a · Questions M1 and M2

Did A keep track of who said what?

The judge checks whether A remembers each person’s statements and responds to their different concerns.

Stronger reply: “Ben set a 500-dollar limit. Chloe needs 100 dollars for testing.” A keeps the two people’s information straight.

Weaker reply: “Chloe set the 500-dollar limit.” A has mixed up who said it. Repeating “Good point” to everyone also gives little evidence that A understood their different needs. Read the exact prompt.

3 of 6 · Source name: Call 2b · Questions M3 and M4

Did A put different people’s ideas together?

The judge checks whether A combines information from several people. It also checks whether A can work through disagreements involving three or more positions, when the meeting contains such a disagreement.

Stronger reply: “With Ben’s 500-dollar limit and Chloe’s 100-dollar testing reserve, we have 400 dollars for the design.” This uses both people’s information.

Weaker reply: “We can spend all 500 dollars on the design.” A has lost Chloe’s constraint. Our small budget example illustrates combining information; it contains no three-way disagreement to assess. Read the exact prompt.

4 of 6 · Source name: Call 2c · Questions M5 and M6

Did A consider the whole group?

The judge checks whether A considers everyone’s needs and handles competing requests sensibly. A should explain conflicts instead of simply following whoever spoke last or pushed hardest.

Example extension: Ben now asks to spend all 500 dollars on the design, while Chloe still needs testing money.

Stronger reply: “Spending all 500 would remove Chloe’s testing budget. Can we keep 100 for testing and use 400 for the design?”

Weaker reply: “Sure, Ben, spend all 500.” That ignores the unresolved conflict with Chloe’s request. Read the exact prompt.

5 of 6 · Source name: Call 3a · Questions G1–G4

Were A’s contributions clear and useful?

The judge asks four broad questions: Could people understand A? Did A answer what was actually said? Did A stay with the current topic? Did A help the group reach a decision or next step?

Stronger reply: “Let’s use a design costing at most 400 dollars and keep 100 for testing. Ben, can you finish the durability test by Friday?”

Weaker reply: “Great ideas, everyone. We should think about this more.” It sounds agreeable but gives the group little to act on. Read the exact prompt.

6 of 6 · Source name: Call 3b

Did A do its particular meeting job well?

This call uses the role chosen in the first call. If A acted as a facilitator, the judge asks whether it moved the agenda along, brought people into the discussion, helped reach decisions, summarized the outcome and treated people fairly.

Stronger facilitator reply: “Ben, can you run the test by Friday? Chloe, does that cover your concern? Then our decision is a 400-dollar design plus 100 for testing.”

Weaker facilitator behavior: A says “Let’s move on” without resolving the budget or asking Chloe about testing. It is organizing the discussion, but doing so poorly.

If the first call instead identified A as a Driver, this call would ask how well it proposed and supported new ideas. A Collaborator is checked on building on others’ ideas; an Evaluator on useful criticism and risks. Read the exact role prompts.

How the pieces fit: Call 1 chooses A’s role. Calls 2–4 grade its ability to work with several people. Call 5 grades basic usefulness. Call 6 grades how well it performed its role. The final score combines the multi-party, basic-usefulness and role scores equally, then applies the participation adjustment described below.

Technical details: how the model’s output becomes judge input

A detailed persona prompt or a minimal alternative tells the speech model how to participate. The judge receives the speaker list, target label A and the written conversation. The adapters save the model’s words with timestamps in an alignments array. The evaluator removes padding tokens, joins text into utterances, and starts a new utterance when a gap exceeds 1.2 seconds. It combines those utterances with the other people’s reference sentences and sorts everything by start time. The written lines sent to the judge omit the timestamps. Technical details: the required answer format

The answer must be JSON with score, positive_evidence, negative_evidence and reasoning for each question. Each English evidence list allows one quote of at most 12 words; each reason allows 40 words. The Chinese limits are 20 characters per quote and 40 characters per reason. Output format and shared rating scale

System message

You are a professional meeting behavior analyst specializing in identifying participant role types.

## Role Definitions

**Facilitator**
Core traits: opens or closes the meeting, actively transitions topics (3+ times), assigns speaking turns, announces or confirms decisions.
Typical language: "Let's move on to the next item", "Could you share your thoughts?", "Let's go with that", "That's all for today."

**Driver**
Core traits: high proportion of independently initiated new ideas, leads discussion direction, puts forward concrete proposals or action recommendations — initiates more than responds.
Typical language: "I suggest…", "We could do it this way…", "I think the direction should be…"

**Collaborator**
Core traits: builds on others' ideas, integrates different opinions, facilitates consensus — extending others' ideas more often than independently initiating.
Typical language: "Building on what you said…", "These two points can be combined…", "I think everyone is actually saying something similar."

**Evaluator**
Core traits: challenges existing proposals, identifies risks or constraints, raises counter-examples — questioning utterances exceed 35% of total contributions.
Typical language: "But there's a problem here…", "What if this leads to…", "There's a premise we haven't discussed."

## Role Priority Rules
Facilitator > Driver > Evaluator > Collaborator (default fallback)

When two roles are clearly satisfied, record both a primary and a secondary role.

## Dimension M1: Multi-Speaker State Tracking

[Definition]
As the meeting progresses, can the participant consistently and accurately distinguish what each person has said and what position they hold, without confusion or misattribution over time?

[Evaluation Points]
- When quoting others, does the participant misattribute statements (attribute A's words to B)?
- When referencing earlier statements later in the meeting, are attributions consistent with the original?
- After multiple people speak consecutively, is the participant still accurate when describing someone's position?
- When there are 4+ participants, does tracking accuracy noticeably decline?

[High Score Signals] Clear attributions that match the original; can clearly distinguish multiple speakers' different positions
[Low Score Signals] Misattribution, position confusion, failure to recall key things someone said earlier

---

## Dimension M2: Differentiated Response Capability

[Definition]
When facing multiple participants with different positions, needs, or backgrounds, can the participant give distinguishably different responses rather than sending identical messages to everyone?

[Evaluation Points]
- Does the participant give different specific responses to participants with different positions?
- Are weaker voices or silent participants ignored?
- When facing disagreement, does the participant avoid real differentiation by offering generic statements like "everyone makes a good point"?

[High Score Signals] Explicitly says different things to different people; while acknowledging A, specifically addresses B's concerns
[Low Score Signals] Sends the same message to the whole group; only responds to the most vocal participant; silent participants are consistently ignored

## Dimension M3: Cross-Speaker Information Integration

[Definition]
When key information is scattered across multiple speakers' contributions, can the participant piece together a complete picture and identify implicit consensus or contradictions that span across different speakers?

[Evaluation Points]
- Do summaries or decisions cover key information from different people, or rely only on the most recent statements?
- Does the participant identify logical contradictions scattered across different speakers and time points?
- Does the participant notice key information from less vocal participants?
- Can the participant recognize when multiple people are expressing the same idea in different words?

[High Score Signals] Summaries trace back to specific statements from different speakers; identifies cross-turn contradictions or latent consensus
[Low Score Signals] Summaries only reflect the later part of the discussion or the most vocal participants; ignores early or peripheral contributions

## Dimension M4: Multi-Party Disagreement Handling

[Definition]
When there are three or more conflicting positions (not just a simple two-party standoff), can the participant identify each party's core concerns and find a mutually acceptable integrative path, rather than simply siding with one party or deferring?

[Evaluation Points]
- When A, B, and C each have different positions, does the participant only handle the most prominent conflict and miss the third party?
- Can the participant identify a proposal that actually contains multiple parties' concerns and present it as an integration point?
- Is the conclusion a substantive convergence, or an empty deferral like "we need to discuss this further"?
- Does the participant show obvious bias toward the party with the most speaking time?

[High Score Signals] Explicitly identifies the core concerns of 3+ parties; finds an integrative direction accommodating multiple parties
[Low Score Signals] Only handles two-party conflict; gives empty deferrals; biases toward the most vocal party

## Dimension M5: Group Awareness in Speaking

[Definition]
Is the participant aware that everything they say is visible to all present in a multi-person context, and do they accordingly consider the impact of their words on different attendees — rather than speaking as if in a one-on-one conversation?

[Evaluation Points]
- When responding to one person, does the participant inadvertently ignore the positions or feelings of others present?
- In front of people with differing positions, does the participant strongly endorse one side without any balancing acknowledgment?
- Does the participant use large amounts of assumed context that only specific people would understand?
- Does the participant leverage the multi-person setting to deliver valuable information to multiple parties in a single utterance?

[High Score Signals] When responding to A, proactively accounts for B and C's needs; uses public statements to advance group consensus
[Low Score Signals] Strongly endorses A's controversial proposal in front of B; uses large amounts of insider language only A would understand

---

## Dimension M6: Noise Resistance and Input Priority Judgment

[Definition]
When multiple participants simultaneously or consecutively send competing inputs, can the participant make reasonable priority judgments rather than simply executing the most recent or loudest instruction?

[Evaluation Points]
- Is the participant completely overridden by the last message, forgetting earlier important instructions or context?
- Does the participant appropriately differentiate priority between the facilitator's agenda instructions and ordinary participants' spontaneous interruptions?
- After one party applies sustained pressure, does the participant change position without substantive justification?
- When facing contradictory inputs, does the participant explicitly flag the conflict rather than quietly siding with one?

[High Score Signals] Explicitly explains priority reasoning; proactively clarifies contradictory inputs rather than unilaterally executing
[Low Score Signals] Gets completely overridden by the last message; changes position without justification after repeated pressure

## Dimension G1: Clarity and Actionability

[Definition] Can the participant's contributions be clearly understood, and do they contain enough concrete information for others to react, decide, or act? Assess both clarity and practical usefulness.

How the released code computes scores

Each judged rating is mapped to 0–100 as shown in Table 4. General score G averages four dimensions; multi-party score M averages six; role score R averages the four or five dimensions of the selected role. The three groups receive equal weight, so the larger multi-party group does not automatically dominate. ScoreCalculator

Participation is computed separately. For either the model or the human reference, r is the time between that target’s first utterance start and last utterance end, divided by the corresponding meeting span. A target with no utterances receives zero. This measures the span of presence, rather than total voiced duration or the fraction of turns spoken. The coefficient c compares model and human spans:

\[c=\max\!\left(0,\,1-\left|r_{\mathrm{model}}-r_{\mathrm{human}}\right|\right)\]

The released implementation multiplies the group average by c. The dataset score averages these per-sample final scores; it does not multiply a dataset-wide mean participation coefficient by independently averaged groups.

\[\text{Sample score}=\frac{G+M+R}{3}\,c\]

Worked arithmetic example, not a measured result: suppose normalized G, M and R are 75, 50 and 25. Their mean is 50. If the model’s participation span ratio is 0.8 and the human’s is 0.9, c is 0.9, so the sample score is 45. P1 is reported separately on a 0–100 scale as 100 times c; the raw result stores it on the 1–5 scale as 1 plus 4 times c. Participation and stored P1

Paper/code discrepancy

Appendix E.5 displays the final score as the equally weighted average of the three judge groups, without a participation multiplier. The pinned released code applies c to every group and to the final score. We document the implementation above; this source review does not establish which exact revision produced the paper’s result table. Paper Appendix E.5 · Executed scoring expression

Limits of the participation metric

Two brief utterances near the beginning and end can produce a span ratio close to one, just as continuous talking can. The metric therefore does not establish an appropriate speech rate, balanced airtime, correct entry points or interruption recovery. Those behaviors would need separate measurements.

What an evaluation result would establish

The scores describe transcript-level meeting behavior under replay of fixed human peers. Those peers cannot respond to a model’s novel proposal, so our interpretation is that this protocol cannot alone establish success in a fully reactive group conversation. The paper explicitly excludes direct latency and interruption-timing measurement. It also provides no dedicated tool-execution, backchannel-timing or acoustic-quality score. Paper §2.2

Long-range tracking and addressee-sensitive behavior are assessed through the rubric questions, not through an additional question set with exact-match gold answers in this scorer. English and Chinese have corresponding templates; bilingual coverage does not by itself establish code-switching ability. The source recordings and aligned utterances below illustrate the data, not evaluated model behavior.

Reproduce the data checks

From the project workspace, download the same pinned snapshot and run the validator and preview exporter:

python scripts/download_assets.py multitalkbench
envs/data/bin/python scripts/validate_multitalk_bench.py
envs/data/bin/python scripts/export_multitalk_bench_preview.py
python site/scripts/build_multitalk_bench.py

The site hosts only two short, attributed excerpts and the integrity record; it does not mirror the full 5.19 GB release into GitHub Pages.
